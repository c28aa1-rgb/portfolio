# Sounding Lines

A design philosophy for the site's artwork.

Sounding Lines treats attention as a kind of survey. A contour is what remains when someone has walked a hillside, dropped a weighted line into a lake, or flown the same valley enough times to know its shape. Each line records a single constant value carried patiently across uneven ground. The work is made of nothing but these records: hundreds of closed and open paths, each one the product of painstaking, meticulous measurement, stacked until the terrain announces itself without ever being drawn directly.

Space is open and cool, like chart paper that has never been folded. The field is never filled edge to edge; it drifts in from one side and thins to silence, so the emptiness reads as water, air, or a page waiting for the next entry. Density carries the meaning. Where lines crowd, the ground is steep and something is happening. Where they part, the eye rests. Composition is balanced by weight of line, not by symmetry, and every gap between strokes is set with the care of a master draftsman who has redrawn the sheet many times.

Color is withheld almost completely. Lines are a single deep ink, the paper a pale grey-green. One accent exists: a small international-orange mark, the color of a windsock or a fishing float, placed exactly once, the way a navigator marks a single waypoint on an otherwise neutral chart. It is never decoration. If it appears twice it means nothing.

Typography whispers. Small monospaced labels sit beside the field like annotations on a survey plate: a depth, a heading, an index. They are clinical, sparse and aligned to an unseen grid. Text never explains the image; it only proves that someone was here, measuring. Every label is placed with deliberate, expert restraint.

The finished work should look like it took countless hours by someone at the very top of the craft: lines crisp at any scale, no stroke out of place, no overlaps that were not intended, margins generous and exact. It is an artifact of patient observation, a quiet argument that curiosity can be mapped.
